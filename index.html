    Safety

    • Batteries only. Never mains power in a circuit.
    • Never plug the raw 9V signal into a DJ mixer, interface or headphones.
    • Hot chip or battery: disconnect and look for a short.
    • Solder in a ventilated room and wash your hands after.
    Workbench

    Build it hole by hole, then switch it on

    A virtual breadboard with the real layout: each numbered column of five holes is connected, and the long red and blue rails carry + and −. Place parts the way you will on your desk; the steps tick themselves off when the connections are right.

    How the workbench works
    1. Pick a level above. Each one is a session from the Curriculum.
    2. Read the orange card. It tells you what to do now and why. The part you need is already selected in the row of buttons.
    3. Click the glowing holes on the board: first click puts the first leg in, second click puts the other leg in. For parts with a + side (LED, 10µF, 470µF), the first click is the + leg.
    4. Watch the list on the right. When the connection is right, the step gets a green tick and the card jumps to the next step.
    5. Made a mistake? Press Undo, or turn on Remove and click the part.

    How a breadboard is wired inside: each numbered column has two groups of five holes (a–e and f–j), and each group is one metal strip. The long red and blue lines at the top and bottom are the + and − rails. Hover or tap any hole to see what's joined to it.

      Tip: hover or tap a hole to see which holes are joined to it. Click a part with Remove on to take it out.

      Simulator

      Try each build before you touch a wire

      Every sim uses the same part values as its real session, so a knob here does what the pot will do on your breadboard. The sound is close, not exact: real chips drift and vary.

      How the simulator works
      1. Each card below is one session from the Curriculum, with the same parts as knobs.
      2. Press Power on a card to hear it. Press it again to stop. Only one card plays at a time.
      3. Move the sliders. They are your pots, capacitors and light sensor; the number next to each one is its real value.
      4. Watch the screen under the knobs. It's an oscilloscope: it draws the sound you hear, so you can see what each knob does to the wave.
      5. Follow the Try this list on each card, top to bottom.

      Start quiet. Square waves are harsh, just like the real thing. Only one sim plays at a time.

      Handbook

      Keep this open while you build

      Units and formulas

      Prefixes: 1k = 1,000 · 1M = 1,000,000 · 1µF = 1,000nF · 1nF = 1,000pF. So 100nF = 0.1µF.

      Ohm's law. Voltage, current and resistance.

      V = I × R

      LED resistor. Supply minus about 2V for the LED, divided by the current you want (5–10mA is plenty).

      R = (Vsupply − 2) ÷ I

      RC time. How long a capacitor takes to mostly fill. Bigger R or C means slower, which means lower pitch.

      t = R × C

      Filter cutoff. Where an RC filter starts cutting.

      fc = 1 ÷ (2π × R × C)

      40106 rule of thumb: double the capacitor (or the resistor) and the pitch drops about an octave.

      Reading part values

      Resistors, 4 bands: first digit, second digit, number of zeros, then tolerance (gold = ±5%). Hold gold on the right. Unsure? Measure it.

      ColorDigit / zerosColorDigit / zeros

      Brown1Blue6

      Yellow4White9

      The three you'll use most: brown-black-red = 1k, brown-black-orange = 10k, brown-black-yellow = 100k.

      Ceramic capacitors use a 3-digit code in pF: two digits, then zeros.

      Electrolytic capacitors print value and max voltage (10µF 25V). Pick a rating above your supply. Pots: B = linear (pitch, controls), A = logarithmic (volume). B1M = 1MΩ linear.

      Chip pinouts

      Notch at the top: pin 1 is top-left; count down the left side, then up the right.

      Pin 16 = +, pin 8 = ground, pin 14 = clock in, pin 13 = ground, pin 15 = reset (to ground, or to a step's pin for a shorter loop). The LM386 module is labeled VCC, GND, IN and speaker +/− on the board.

      When it doesn't work

      Go through these in order; most problems are the first three.

      1. Power: fresh battery? About 9V between the chip's + and ground pins?
      2. Rails: are the breadboard's power rails connected? Some split in the middle.
      3. Chip direction: notch in the right place, straddling the center gap?
      4. Rows: is each leg in the row you think? One hole off is the classic mistake.
      5. Ground: does everything, amp module included, share ground?
      6. Unused inputs on the 40106 grounded?
      7. Polarity: electrolytics, LEDs and diodes the right way round?
      8. Values: measure resistors, read capacitor codes.
      9. Too slow to hear? Put an LED on the output to see it blink.
      10. Hot part? Disconnect power and look for a short.

      Glossary

      WordMeaning
      OscillatorA circuit that flips back and forth by itself; makes a tone
      VCOVoltage-controlled oscillator: pitch follows a control voltage
      LFOLow-frequency oscillator: too slow to hear, moves other things
      Control voltage (CV)A voltage that controls pitch, volume or cutoff instead of being heard
      Filter / cutoffRemoves some frequencies; cutoff is where it starts
      ResonanceA boost right at the cutoff; makes the filter sing
      VCAVoltage-controlled amplifier: volume follows a control voltage
      EnvelopeA shape over time (rise, fall) started by a gate
      Gate / triggerAn on-off pulse that starts something
      ClockA steady stream of pulses that keeps time
      SequencerSteps through a list of values in a loop
      Square waveThe 40106's sound: jumps between high and low; buzzy
      DC blockingA capacitor that passes sound but blocks steady voltage
      GroundThe shared 0V reference
      Dual supply (±V)Plus and minus voltage around ground; op-amps like it
      Breadboard / stripboardTemporary board without solder / permanent board with solder
